The Industrial Revolution: An Overview. Between about 1760 and 1840 Britain changed from a country of farms and workshops into the first industrial nation. Historians call this change the Industrial Revolution. Work that had been done by hand at home moved into factories, where machines driven by water and later by steam produced goods in great quantities. The new methods spread from textiles to iron, coal, transport and many other trades. Output grew faster than ever before, towns grew into cities, and the daily life of millions of people changed. The changes were not sudden: they built on a long period of growth in trade, farming and science. Yet by 1850 more people in Britain worked in industry and trade than in agriculture, which had never happened anywhere in the world before.

Why Britain was first. Several conditions came together in Britain in the eighteenth century. The Agricultural Revolution, with crop rotation and the enclosure of open fields, fed a growing population with fewer farm workers, so labour was free for industry. Britain had large deposits of coal and iron ore, often close to each other and to the sea. Overseas trade and the colonies supplied raw cotton and markets for finished goods, and merchants had capital to invest. Banks and a stable government protected property and contracts. A culture of practical invention, supported by societies such as the Lunar Society of Birmingham, encouraged people to improve machines and to profit from patents.

Textiles: the first factory industry. Cotton was the leading industry of the revolution. In 1764 James Hargreaves invented the spinning jenny, which let one worker spin eight threads at once. In 1769 Richard Arkwright patented the water frame, a spinning machine driven by a water wheel; in 1771 he opened Cromford Mill in Derbyshire, often called the first modern factory. Samuel Crompton combined the two ideas in his spinning mule in 1779, which made fine and strong yarn. Edmund Cartwright built a power loom in 1785, so weaving could also be done by machines. The factory system brought workers, machines and power together under one roof, with fixed hours and strict discipline.

Steam power. The first practical steam engine was built by Thomas Newcomen in 1712 to pump water out of mines, but it wasted much fuel. In 1769 James Watt patented a separate condenser that made the engine far more efficient. From 1775 Watt worked in partnership with the manufacturer Matthew Boulton in Birmingham, and in the 1780s their engines were adapted to produce rotary motion. Rotary steam engines could drive the machines of a factory, so mills no longer had to stand beside fast rivers. Factories could now be built in towns close to coal, workers and markets. By 1800 about 500 Boulton and Watt engines were at work in Britain.

Iron and coal. Iron was needed for machines, engines, bridges and rails. In 1709 Abraham Darby began to smelt iron ore with coke made from coal instead of charcoal at Coalbrookdale, which freed the industry from the shrinking forests. In 1779 the first iron bridge in the world was opened over the river Severn near Coalbrookdale. In 1784 Henry Cort patented the puddling and rolling process, which produced wrought iron in large amounts. Coal mining grew to feed steam engines, ironworks and homes: British coal output rose from about 3 million tons in 1700 to about 30 million tons by 1830. Deeper mines needed steam pumps, and so coal and steam grew together.

Transport: canals and railways. Heavy goods such as coal were expensive to move on poor roads. The Bridgewater Canal, opened in 1761, carried coal from the Duke of Bridgewater's mines to Manchester and halved the price of coal in the town. A canal boom followed, and by 1830 England had about 4000 miles of canals. Railways were the next step. The Stockton and Darlington Railway opened in 1825 as the first public railway to use steam locomotives. In 1829 George and Robert Stephenson's locomotive Rocket won the Rainhill Trials, and in 1830 the Liverpool and Manchester Railway became the first intercity line for passengers and goods. By 1850 Britain had more than 6000 miles of railway.

Social effects. Industrial towns grew very fast: Manchester grew from about 25000 people in 1772 to more than 300000 by 1850. Workers lived in crowded, badly built housing without clean water or sewers, and diseases such as cholera spread quickly. In the factories people worked twelve to fourteen hours a day, and many children worked in mills and mines. Some workers resisted the machines: between 1811 and 1816 the Luddites broke knitting frames and power looms. Reformers pressed Parliament to act. The Factory Act of 1833 limited the hours of children in textile mills and created the first factory inspectors. Later laws restricted work in the mines and shortened the working day.

Spread and legacy. From Britain the new methods spread to Belgium, France, the German states and the United States in the first half of the nineteenth century, often with British machines, engineers and capital. After about 1870 a second industrial revolution based on steel, chemicals, electricity and oil began. Historians still debate how quickly the living standards of ordinary workers improved: real wages rose only slowly before the 1840s, but in the long run industrialisation made possible a much higher standard of living. The Industrial Revolution also created a new working class and a new middle class, and it began the large use of fossil fuels that shapes the world today.
